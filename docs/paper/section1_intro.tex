\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The Border Gateway Protocol (BGP) is the de facto inter-domain
routing protocol of the Internet. It exchanges prefix reachability
information between autonomous systems (ASes) and determines which
prefixes each network can reach and through which path
\cite{rfc4271}. BGP was designed around local routing policy and
administrative autonomy, which allows independently operated networks
to make their own routing decisions. The protocol itself, however,
does not establish that an AS that advertises a prefix is authorised
to originate it, nor does it guarantee that an AS-path is consistent
with the commercial relationships between ASes. Consequently, invalid
announcements can propagate across multiple networks before they are
detected or filtered.

Routing security mechanisms, including RPKI with Route Origin
Validation and the operational practices promoted by MANRS, can
prevent or limit some classes of invalid announcements
\cite{cooper-2017-rpki,manrs}. Their deployment nevertheless remains
incomplete and uneven, and even correctly detected routing anomalies
must still be interpreted in the context of their operational impact.
Routing changes can remove reachability, redirect traffic through
unintended paths, or expose prefixes to networks that should not
transit them. These changes may result from prefix hijacks, route
leaks, large withdrawal events, or legitimate traffic engineering.
Anomaly detection therefore addresses only the first part of the
operator's problem: a routing alert does not by itself establish
whether the event affects service or business traffic.

Operators rely on BGP monitoring platforms to detect routing
anomalies. These platforms compare observed announcements, origins,
and paths with maintained policies or baselines, and generate alerts
when deviations occur. Data-plane monitoring systems provide
complementary evidence in the form of traffic volumes, request rates,
and service-level measurements. This combination is necessary, but it
also creates a substantial triage burden. Monitoring platforms can
emit far more alerts than operators can review individually, and an
alert indicates only that a monitored signal deviated from its
baseline; it does not establish that the event affects service or
business traffic. An alert may reflect legitimate traffic
engineering, temporary routing churn, maintenance activity, or a
traffic change caused by independent user or application behaviour.
The resulting \emph{alert flood} can delay the detection of high-impact
incidents and consume operator attention with events that have no
business impact.

The difficulty is not confined to excessive alert volume. Routing and
traffic signals are generally analysed in isolation. Routing monitors
describe path changes, prefix withdrawals, and policy violations,
whereas traffic monitors describe volume deviations and service-level
changes. Neither type of monitor directly answers the question an
operator actually faces: \emph{did this routing change affect traffic,
and is the observed impact attributable to routing?} A routing
anomaly may produce no measurable traffic change when the affected
prefix carries little traffic, while an observed traffic drop may be
caused by a non-routing failure. Conversely, hundreds of routing
alerts can arise from a single root event, and several routing changes
may overlap with a traffic anomaly without being causally related to
it. Manual post-mortem analysis across routing and traffic teams is
therefore slow, difficult to reproduce, and poorly suited to
minute-level triage.

These observations motivate a system that joins control-plane and
data-plane evidence at the point of triage, rather than leaving their
interpretation to a manual post-mortem. The system should preserve the
evidence behind each verdict, distinguish an anomaly from an absence
of telemetry, and represent uncertainty explicitly. Its objective is
to identify the small set of events that warrant operator attention
without making unsupported causal claims from weak or conflicting
evidence. This shift from independent alerts to auditable incident
hypotheses defines the research problem addressed in this paper.

Realising this objective requires addressing three technical
challenges.

\paragraph{C1 --- Responsiveness versus evidence depth.}
Operators need a rapid assessment when an alert arrives, but a
reliable joint verdict requires traffic baselines, routing updates,
multiple detectors, temporal alignment, and heterogeneous evidence.
Running the complete analysis path for every alert increases both
latency and computational cost, precisely when a quick triage
decision is most valuable.

\paragraph{C2 --- Heterogeneous and data-dependent traffic behaviour.}
AS-level traffic differs substantially across networks. Large ISPs
often exhibit regular daily and weekly patterns, whereas content
networks, enterprise networks, and networks with sparse measurement
coverage can be highly bursty or irregular. A fixed baseline may
therefore produce frequent false positives for volatile networks and
miss genuine outages in series that lack stable periodicity.

\paragraph{C3 --- Directional attribution without a causal graph.}
Temporal proximity between routing and traffic anomalies does not
establish causality. Both may result from a common operational event,
and their observation times may differ because of propagation,
aggregation, and collection delays. Classical methods also rely on
restrictive assumptions: Granger causality depends on predictive
structure and stationarity \cite{granger-1969}, transfer entropy
requires sufficient samples for reliable dependency estimation
\cite{schreiber-2000-te}, and PC addresses conditional independence
rather than directly testing sufficiency \cite{spirtes-cps-2000}.
Pearl's probability of necessity and sufficiency (PNS) provides a
counterfactual formulation, but its identification generally requires
a known causal graph or additional structural assumptions
\cite{pearl-causality-2009,tian-pearl-2000}. BGP telemetry provides no
such graph, and many incident windows are short, sparse, and affected
by unobserved operational decisions.

We address these challenges with three corresponding design
decisions.

\paragraph{S1 --- A two-phase quick-to-deep analysis pipeline
(Addresses C1).}
BGP Tracer separates time-critical triage from deeper evidence
synthesis. Phase~1 collects and aligns routing and traffic telemetry,
runs deterministic detectors, and stores the resulting evidence in an
auditable cache. It produces one of five core verdicts:
\texttt{real\_incident}\allowbreak, \texttt{silent\_alert}\allowbreak,
\texttt{traffic\_only}\allowbreak, \texttt{correlation\_uncertain}\allowbreak,
or \texttt{no\_clear\_anomaly}.
Missing telemetry is reported separately
from an observed absence of anomalies. Phase~2 can consume this cache
for deeper causal analysis and report generation without repeating
the complete data-collection path. This division allows routine
triage to follow a lightweight path while preserving the evidence
needed for follow-up analysis.

\paragraph{S2 --- Reliability-aware traffic anomaly detection
(Addresses C2).}
Rather than excluding irregular or non-Tier-1 ASes, BGP Tracer selects
its traffic baseline according to the available historical data.
When repeated observations support a stable daily or weekly pattern,
the detector constructs a periodic baseline on a shared normalisation
scale. Otherwise, it uses a non-periodic detection path based on
robust statistics. The detector combines these comparisons with
multi-scale and contextual checks, so that ordinary periodic variation
is treated differently from a sustained deviation. This design retains
coverage of heterogeneous networks while reducing false alarms caused
by normal traffic variability.

\paragraph{S3 --- Admissibility-based directional attribution
(Addresses C3).}
BGP Tracer reads the direction of an effect from the order of two onsets
rather than from the size of two contrasts. Each series is segmented
independently, the onset of the selected change is reported as an
\emph{interval} rather than a point, and a direction is committed only
when four vetoes pass: the onset intervals are disjoint and ordered
(precedence); the selected model beats a no-change model with a
significant split statistic (persistence); the response does not move
this much on its own against placebo onsets (exclusivity); and the
observation covers the affected entities (provenance), with a mirror
check that a reversion is answered by a reversion (propagation). The
conjunction is logical, so no weight, prior or floor binds the
conditions, and every refusal returns the veto that failed together with
the statistic behind it. Granger prediction, transfer entropy and
cascade-scope estimation therefore enter as comparators rather than as
adjudicators. \S\ref{sec:method-vetoes} defines the vetoes and
\S\ref{sec:eval-direction} measures the size and power of the resulting
test on planted mechanisms.

\paragraph{S4 --- An auditable agent coordination layer.}
BGP Tracer's analysis is carried by a layer of specialised agents that
exchange typed records through a shared Evidence Blackboard rather than
through free text. The Blackboard applies write-once semantics to
telemetry, so a later stage cannot silently replace an earlier stage's
measurement, and it stores the provenance of every observation, claim, and
contradiction. The layer is designed so that a failed component reduces
the richness of the report without altering the verdict, and so that the
artefact an operator reads can be traced back to the evidence that
produced it. We distinguish the agents that close a genuine
decision--action--observation loop from those that execute a fixed
procedure, and we measure the layer against a monolithic single-prompt
baseline on identical evidence.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A two-phase routing--traffic analysis system.} We design
and implement BGP Tracer, which aligns control-plane and data-plane
evidence, produces an early five-class triage verdict, and preserves a
reusable evidence cache for deeper causal analysis and reporting.

\item \textbf{A reliability-aware traffic detection method for
heterogeneous ASes.} Our detector adapts its baseline to periodic or
non-periodic traffic behaviour, avoiding the blanket exclusion of
irregular ASes while reducing false alarms from ordinary temporal
variation.

\item \textbf{An admissibility-based directional attribution method.} We
report each onset as an interval rather than a point, order the two
edges only when those intervals are disjoint, and admit a direction only
if four vetoes pass: precedence, persistence, exclusivity against
placebo onsets, and provenance and mirror checks. Each refusal returns
the veto that failed together with the statistic behind it, the size and
power of the component tests are measured separately on planted
mechanisms, and the method abstains when the available data cannot
identify a direction.

\item \textbf{An auditable agent coordination layer with measured
degradation behaviour.} Specialised agents exchange typed records
through a shared Evidence Blackboard with write-once semantics and
stored provenance. We characterise which agents close a genuine
decision--action--observation loop, show that injected component
failures leave the verdict unchanged while degrading only the
richness of the report, and show on a 39-event ground-truth corpus
that the layer raises cause accuracy over a monolithic single-prompt
baseline on identical evidence ($0.56$ against $0.10$), answers
$5.6$ of six operator audit questions from typed records where the
baseline answers $1.7$, and---by ablating each coordination stage
separately and repeating the sweep---that its decision-carrying work is
concentrated in one stage rather than spread across the number of its
stages.
\end{enumerate}
