% Section 2 -- Background
\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

Operators use BGP monitoring platforms, such as RIPE RIS and BGPWatch, to
detect changes in routing announcements, origins, and paths, and
traffic-monitoring platforms to detect departures from expected traffic
behaviour. Both detect deviations well, but the resulting alert streams
are far too large for operators to inspect individually:
Figure~\ref{fig:alert-volume} shows GRIP peaking at 2{,}708 monthly
alerts in May 2026 and BGPWatch at 1{,}296 in October 2025, with a
combined monthly range of 608--2{,}799 alerts.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{figs/fig_monthly_alerts.png}
\caption{Monthly alert volume generated by GRIP and BGPWatch from
September 2025 to September 2026. Counts reflect platform-specific
alert definitions and are not incident or false-positive counts.}
\label{fig:alert-volume}
\end{figure}

Alert volume is not incident volume. One routing event can generate many
per-prefix alerts, a high count can arise from legitimate traffic
engineering or maintenance, and many alerts carry no measurable business
impact: AS-path prepending triggers path-change alerts despite being
routine, and a routing anomaly may produce only silent alerts when the
affected prefixes carry little traffic. Conversely, a traffic anomaly may
be caused by demand shift or a non-routing failure and have no routing
cause at all. The operational problem is therefore not to generate fewer
alerts but to determine which correspond to business-relevant routing
incidents, how widely their effects propagate, and which to review first.
That requires joint analysis: routing telemetry gives the affected
prefixes and the propagation scope, traffic telemetry gives whether the
event produced a genuine data-plane impact.

We formulate this as an evidence-based alert-prioritisation problem. For
candidate window $i$, let $\mathcal{R}_i$ and $\mathcal{T}_i$ be the
routing and traffic observations and $\mathcal{A}_{R,i}$, $\mathcal{A}_{T,i}$
the alarms the two monitors produced. The input is
\[
    \mathbf{x}_i = (\mathcal{R}_i,\mathcal{T}_i,\mathcal{A}_{R,i},\mathcal{A}_{T,i}),
\]
and the output is
\begin{equation}
    \Phi(\mathbf{x}_i) = (\hat{v}_i,\hat{d}_i,\hat{c}_i,\hat{s}_i,\mathcal{E}_i),
    \label{eq:problem}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{v}_i$ is the routing--traffic correlation verdict
distinguishing an actionable incident, a silent routing alert, a
traffic-only anomaly, an uncertain correlation, no clear anomaly, or
insufficient telemetry; $\hat{d}_i$ is the estimated causal direction
when the evidence supports one; $\hat{c}_i$ is the incident type
(outage, route leak, origin hijack); $\hat{s}_i\in[0,1]$ is an
evidence-based impact score derived from the observed traffic deviation
and the routing blast radius; and $\mathcal{E}_i$ records the detector
outputs, temporal alignment, significance statistics, and missing-data
conditions used to produce the decision.

The objective of BGP Tracer is to instantiate $\Phi$ so that it
concentrates large and heterogeneous alert streams into a smaller, ranked
set of business-relevant incidents, each traceable to its supporting
evidence, with low-impact or insufficiently supported alerts retained
under an explicit status rather than silently discarded.
